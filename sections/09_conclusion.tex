\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studies macroeconomic nowcasting as a sequence of forecasting
problems indexed by the information available at the forecast origin.  Using a
common pseudo-real-time framework for U.S. GDP, inflation, and labour-market
targets, the analysis separates four questions that are often conflated in
retrospective forecast comparisons: whether incoming information improves a
fixed model, whether a pre-specified stage-dependent model policy adds value
beyond that information-arrival effect, whether historical data vintages
change measured performance, and whether forecast uncertainty can be
calibrated using only errors that were observable before the forecast date.
The design freezes policy choices before the confirmatory evaluation sample and
preserves historical release masks in the latest-vintage counterfactual.

The empirical results reject a simple universal ranking of stages or models.
For H1, later stages have lower mean squared loss in 16 of 28 adjacent
comparisons, with four statistically significant improvements and two
significant deteriorations at the 5\% level.  For H2, the frozen stage policy
differs from the fixed comparator in 11 target--stage cells, has lower mean
squared loss in seven of them, and produces four significant gains---all in the
labour domain---with no significant policy loss.  H3 shows that data revision
can materially alter evaluation without having a uniform sign: the
latest-vintage counterfactual substantially improves unemployment loss, while
core PCE absolute-loss evaluation moves significantly in the opposite
direction and most other target-level effects are weaker.  H4 similarly
favours a conditional interpretation of uncertainty: prior-only empirical
calibration produces strong interval-score gains for labour and core CPI, but
GDP's 100\% empirical coverage is conservative relative to the nominal 80\%
rate and does not imply uniformly superior sharpness.

The frozen robustness exercises sharpen these conclusions without reversing
them. Replacing the H1 fixed comparator preserves the non-monotonic
information-arrival pattern; shortening H2 policy development changes only 2
of 36 stage mappings and no fixed comparator; and the H3 stage decomposition
shows that the UNRATE improvement and core-PCE deterioration are broad across
their respective release cycles rather than single-stage artefacts. H4 is
more tuning-sensitive for GDP, where both alternative rolling windows improve
mean interval scores relative to the 20-observation baseline, while the
inflation conclusions are locally insensitive to the tested half-lives. Holm
adjustment materially contracts the number of significant H1--H3 cells but
leaves the strongest UNRATE and average-hourly-earnings findings intact.

Taken together, the results suggest that the main advantage of a
stage-dependent forecasting architecture is not continual model switching for
its own sake.  The more fundamental requirement is to make the information
boundary explicit.  Forecast performance changes because the observable data
change, because the historical values of those data can later be revised, and,
in selected cases, because a different forecasting rule is better suited to a
particular release stage.  The H1--H2 decomposition makes those mechanisms
separately measurable.  The H3 and H4 exercises extend the same principle to
data vintages and uncertainty: a real-time forecast should be evaluated using
only values, outcomes, and calibration errors that were actually observable at
the relevant date.

The conclusions remain conditional on a finite U.S. evaluation sample,
pre-specified stage definitions, a limited candidate-model universe, and
symmetric empirical interval rules.  In particular, the GDP sample is short,
the confirmatory cell-level tests are unadjusted even though the secondary
Holm analysis documents their multiplicity sensitivity, and the latest-vintage
experiment is not a complete first-release-versus-final-release reconstruction
for every series. The robustness results also show that the GDP interval
conclusions are sensitive to calibration-memory length, so the frozen
20-observation window should not be interpreted as uniquely preferred.  Future
work should test broader model universes and alternative stage partitions,
allow asymmetric or state-dependent predictive distributions, and compare the
frozen policy with a strictly prior-only adaptive challenger.  Prospective evaluation on data accumulated after the present
freeze would provide the strongest test of whether the stage-dependent
relationships documented here remain stable out of sample.
