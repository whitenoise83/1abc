\section{Empirical Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Sample, forecast counts, and confirmatory evaluation set}
\label{subsec:results_sample}

The confirmatory evaluation begins in 2023Q2 for GDP and in February 2023 for
inflation and labour-market targets.  GDP therefore contributes 12 evaluation
quarters at each of its five release stages.  Monthly inflation and labour
samples contain approximately 38--41 observations per target--stage cell,
with the small differences arising from endpoint availability and the
predeclared exclusion of adjacent-stage H1 comparisons that share an identical
information-set hash.  All H1 and H2 inference uses the frozen evaluation
sample only; policy development and the embargo observation precede this
sample.  H3 uses the same evaluation origins under matched real-time and
latest-vintage masks, while its target-level summary averages only over
target periods with a complete set of stages.  H4 applies the frozen point
policy to the same evaluation period and contains 1,302 eligible primary
interval forecasts.

\subsection{H1: the marginal predictive value of incoming information}
\label{subsec:h1_results}

H1 holds the forecasting model fixed and asks whether the next release stage
reduces loss.  The result is not a monotone ``more information is always
better'' pattern.  Across the 28 adjacent-stage target comparisons, the mean
squared-loss differential is negative in 16 cases and positive in 12.  Using
the archived two-sided HAC test at the 5\% level, four transitions show a
significant squared-loss improvement and two show a significant
deterioration.

The significant improvements are concentrated in specific parts of the
release calendar.  Core CPI improves from month open to mid-month
(\(p=0.0137\)); headline PCE improves from mid-month to month end
(\(p=0.0472\)); core PCE also improves from mid-month to month end
(\(p=0.0121\)); and unemployment improves sharply from month end to the
pre-employment-report stage (\(p=0.0013\)).  In contrast, headline PCE
deteriorates from month end to pre-release (\(p=0.0195\)), and average hourly
earnings deteriorates from after week 1 to after week 2 (\(p=0.0186\)).
The MAE results reinforce the non-monotonic interpretation: they identify
additional deteriorations for GDP and headline CPI at particular transitions,
as well as a PAYEMS improvement from after week 2 to month end.

These results provide only qualified support for H1.  Incoming information is
predictively valuable at several economically interpretable release points,
but the marginal value of a release depends on the target and on what is
already in the information set.  Later cutoffs can also increase error, so H1
should not be interpreted as a deterministic ordering of forecast accuracy by
calendar stage.

\subsection{H2: stage-dependent policies versus fixed-model forecasting}
\label{subsec:h2_results}

H2 asks whether the frozen stage policy adds value beyond the information
arrival documented in H1.  In 25 of the 36 target--stage cells, the
development-sample policy selects exactly the same model as the frozen
target-level fixed comparator; those cells are mechanical ties and contain no
information about the incremental value of stage-dependent model choice.  The
remaining 11 cells are the informative H2 comparisons.

The stage policy has lower mean squared error in seven of those 11 cells and
higher mean squared error in four.  Four stage-policy gains are statistically
significant at the 5\% level, all in the labour domain, and no stage-policy
loss is statistically significant.  For average hourly earnings, switching
from the fixed Bridge Ridge model to the Equal-Weight Ensemble lowers squared
loss at month end (\(p=0.0012\)) and before the employment report
(\(p=0.00036\)).  For PAYEMS, the development-selected 12-Month Mean improves
on Bridge Ridge at month open (\(p=0.0092\)).  For unemployment, AR(1)
strongly improves on the fixed Equal-Weight Ensemble at month open
(\(p<0.0001\)).  MAE inference points in the same direction for all four
cells.

The confirmatory evidence therefore supports a narrower version of H2 than a
claim of universal stage-policy dominance.  Stage-dependent model choice adds
clear predictive value in several labour-market cells, especially at month
open and late in the earnings release cycle.  The GDP and inflation policy
changes do not produce statistically distinguishable squared-loss gains in
the evaluation sample.  The absence of significant policy losses is
reassuring, but it does not by itself establish broad superiority because
most target--stage cells are same-model ties.

\subsection{H3: the effect of data vintages on forecast evaluation}
\label{subsec:h3_results}

H3 compares the frozen stage-policy forecasts generated from historically
available data with a latest-vintage counterfactual that preserves the
historical observation mask and forecast origin.  The archived loss
differential is defined as latest-vintage loss minus real-time loss, so
negative values indicate that revision to the latest vintage improves
measured forecast accuracy.

The vintage effect is strongly target dependent.  At the target level,
unemployment is the clearest case: latest-vintage data reduce the
stage-averaged squared-loss differential by 0.0176
(\(p=1.46\times10^{-7}\)) and the absolute-loss differential by 0.0294
(\(p=2.19\times10^{-6}\)).  GDP also has a negative mean squared-loss
differential, but the evidence is only suggestive (\(p=0.102\)).  Core CPI
leans in the same direction, particularly under absolute loss
(\(p=0.063\)).  Conversely, the PCE measures show that revision need not make
a historical forecast exercise look better: core PCE has a positive
stage-averaged absolute-loss differential of 0.0678 with \(p=0.0487\), while
headline PCE is also positive but does not reject equality at 5\%.

H3 therefore rejects a simple view in which data revision induces a uniform
directional bias in forecast evaluation.  Vintage choice can materially alter
measured performance---most clearly for unemployment---but the sign and size
of the effect vary across targets.  The confirmatory H3 design evaluates the
frozen selected stage-policy forecast and its loss; candidate-model ranking
sensitivity is outside the present analysis and is not reported as an H3
result.

\subsection{H4: predictive-interval calibration and sharpness}
\label{subsec:h4_results}

The confirmatory H4 experiment contains 1,302 primary interval forecasts
across the GDP, inflation, and labour target--stage cells.  All evaluation
observations satisfy the predeclared minimum calibration history.  The
results support prior-only empirical calibration as a useful uncertainty
procedure, but they do not support a universal ranking of interval methods
across domains or information stages.

GDP is the clearest example of the distinction between coverage and
sharpness.  The rolling empirical interval covers all 12 evaluation
observations at every GDP stage.  This 100\% coverage is conservative rather
than ideally calibrated: the unconditional-coverage test rejects the nominal
80\% rate at each stage (\(p=0.0207\)).  Relative to the Gaussian RMSE
benchmark, rolling \(q_{0.8}\) has a significantly lower interval score at
the early-quarter and after-month-1 stages, but significantly higher scores
after month 2, at quarter end, and immediately before the advance release.
Hence the preferred GDP interval benchmark reverses as the quarter matures.

Inflation coverage is closer to the nominal rate.  Across stages, primary
coverage ranges from 0.850 to 0.923 for headline CPI, 0.872 to 0.900 for core
CPI, 0.872 to 0.921 for headline PCE, and 0.868 to 0.900 for core PCE.
The strongest sharpness result is for core CPI: the exponentially weighted
interval has a significantly lower mean interval score than the Gaussian
benchmark at all four stages and than rolling \(q_{0.8}\) at the final three
stages.  Headline CPI, headline PCE, and core PCE show smaller and less
uniform score differences.  Month-open headline CPI and headline PCE
overcover sufficiently for the unconditional-coverage test to reject at the
5\% level, but the remaining inflation stage cells do not show a broad
pattern of coverage-test rejection.

Labour provides the strongest evidence for exponentially weighted calibration.  Primary
coverage ranges from 0.805 to 0.854 for average hourly earnings, from 0.878
to 0.927 for payroll employment, and from 0.750 to 0.975 for unemployment.
For both earnings and payroll employment, the exponentially weighted
interval has a significantly lower mean interval score than both the
Gaussian and rolling empirical benchmarks at every stage.  For unemployment,
it significantly improves on the Gaussian benchmark at all five stages, but
its advantage over rolling \(q_{0.8}\) is confined to month open.  The
unemployment interval also displays the strongest stage dependence in
calibration: month-open coverage is 0.975, whereas coverage falls to 0.750
after week 2 and at month end before returning to exactly 0.800 immediately
before the employment report.  Violation independence is rejected at the
after-week-2 and month-end stages (\(p=0.0253\)), although the corresponding
joint conditional-coverage tests are just above the 5\% threshold.

Taken together, H4 receives qualified support.  Prior-only empirical
calibration frequently improves probabilistic forecast scores, especially
for labour and core CPI, while GDP demonstrates that high raw coverage can
reflect excessive width and that the preferred calibration method may change
with the information stage.  The evidence therefore favours
domain- and stage-aware uncertainty calibration rather than a single
universally dominant interval rule.
