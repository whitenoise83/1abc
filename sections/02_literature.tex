\section{Related Literature and Economic Background}
\label{sec:literature}

The empirical design developed in this paper draws on several mature strands of
the macroeconomic forecasting literature. Real-time-data research establishes
that forecasts should be constructed from historically feasible information
sets rather than retrospectively completed datasets. The nowcasting and
mixed-frequency literature studies how forecasts evolve as information arrives
within the target period. Factor, shrinkage, and regularisation methods provide
alternative ways of exploiting large predictor sets, while forecast-combination
and model-instability research addresses uncertainty over which forecasting
specification should be preferred. The contribution of the present paper is
therefore not any one of these ingredients in isolation. The relevant question
is how they can be organised in a controlled pseudo-real-time experiment that
separates information arrival from stage-dependent model choice.


% ============================================================
\subsection{Real-time macroeconomic forecasting}
\label{subsec:lit_realtime}
% ============================================================

A defining feature of real-time macroeconomic forecasting is that the
information set available to the forecaster evolves asynchronously. Economic
indicators differ in frequency, publication delay, and revision policy, so a
dataset observed at an actual forecast origin typically has a ragged edge rather
than the balanced rectangular structure commonly found in ex-post databases.
The modern nowcasting literature formalises this problem by treating incoming
macroeconomic releases as sequential additions to the information set and by
updating the assessment of current economic conditions as new information
arrives \citep{giannone2008nowcasting,banbura2013realtimeflow}. Fully real-time
applications demonstrate that this data flow can be tracked at considerably
higher frequency than the conventional monthly or quarterly forecasting
horizon. For example, \citet{liebermann2014realtime} reconstructs historical
information sufficiently precisely to follow U.S. GDP nowcasts through the
daily release calendar.

The release-calendar problem is distinct from the problem of data revisions.
A historical forecaster does not observe the complete revised history currently
stored in a statistical database. Instead, the available observations belong
to particular historical vintages. The real-time dataset constructed by
\citet{croushorestark2001realtime} made this distinction operational by storing
successive snapshots of the macroeconomic information available at different
dates. Using such data, \citet{starkcroushore2002forecasting} show that model
specification and measured forecast accuracy can be sensitive to the vintage
used in the exercise. Thus a pseudo-out-of-sample forecast based on today's
revised history is not generally equivalent to the forecast that could actually
have been produced at the historical forecast origin.

The treatment of historical observations within the estimation sample also
matters. \citet{koenig2003useabuse} distinguish alternative procedures for
constructing forecasting equations from real-time data and show that the choice
can materially affect out-of-sample performance. The broader literature
therefore treats vintage construction not simply as a data-management detail
but as part of the econometric design
\citep{croushore2011frontiers}. Real-time evaluation does not imply that revised
data necessarily make every forecasting conclusion misleading, nor that every
revision is economically large. Rather, it implies that conclusions about
predictive performance should not inadvertently depend on observations that
were unavailable when the forecast would have been made.

Recent forecasting exercises reinforce this point while simultaneously
highlighting a separate role for the within-period information flow.
\citet{heinrichreif2025realtime}, for example, use ALFRED vintages to evaluate
mixed-frequency models for U.S. GDP, industrial production, CPI inflation, and
unemployment over several intra-quarter information sets. Their framework
therefore combines historical ALFRED vintages with changing within-quarter
availability. Conceptually, however, the two dimensions remain different.
Release stage determines \emph{which observations are observable}, whereas data
vintage determines \emph{which historically recorded values of those
observations are available}. This distinction underlies the later separation in
this paper between the information-arrival experiment and the
real-time-versus-latest-vintage counterfactual.

\subsection{Mixed-frequency and bridge-equation nowcasting}
\label{subsec:lit_mixed_frequency}
% ============================================================

The mixed-frequency literature provides several ways to exploit indicators that
are observed more frequently and earlier than the target variable. Bridge
equations constitute one of the most established approaches. Monthly indicators
are first completed or forecast where necessary, aggregated to the target
frequency, and then linked to the lower-frequency variable through relatively
parsimonious forecasting equations. Their continued practical appeal reflects
their transparency and their ability to exploit timely indicators without
estimating a large multivariate system. Importantly for the present paper,
bridge systems have long been implemented in a release-calendar setting.
\citet{drechselmaurin2011flow} construct six successive forecasts within the
quarter, with the available indicators, individual bridge equations, and
combination weights differing as the information set evolves.

The informational value of a predictor therefore depends partly on its
publication timing rather than only on its contemporaneous correlation with the
target. \citet{banbura2011blackbox} show that publication lags are important for
understanding the contribution of hard and soft indicators to GDP forecasts:
relatively timely survey and financial information can have substantial value
early in the quarter, before more direct activity measures are observed.
Consequently, evaluating a predictor after imposing a balanced ex-post dataset
can misrepresent its usefulness in the actual forecasting problem.

MIDAS and mixed-frequency multivariate models provide alternative solutions to
the same temporal mismatch. \citet{schumacher2016midasbridge} shows that MIDAS
and bridge equations differ in their treatment of high-frequency lag weights,
multi-step forecasting, and the way current-period high-frequency information
enters estimation. Mixed-frequency VARs instead retain variables at their
original sampling frequencies within a joint dynamic system.
\citet{schorfheide2015mixedfrequency} demonstrate in a real-time U.S. application
that information becoming available within the quarter can improve predictions
relative to models based only on quarterly-frequency information.
\citet{carriero2015realtime} similarly develop a Bayesian mixed-frequency model
that can incorporate a changing set of within-quarter monthly observations while
generating both point and density nowcasts.

These studies establish that the value of both information and forecasting
methods can vary over the release cycle. In a fully real-time U.S. GDP exercise,
\citet{aastveit2014density} update competing model classes as individual
releases arrive and find that relative model rankings change during the
quarter. \citet{liebermann2014realtime} likewise finds that nowcast precision
generally improves as the real-time data flow advances.
More recently, \citet{heinrichreif2025realtime} report material differences in
forecast accuracy across three intra-quarter information sets, while
\citet{kantpickwinter2025ml} find that the relative performance of econometric
and machine-learning approaches varies across pseudo-real-time forecasting and
nowcasting positions. Accordingly, stage-by-stage forecast evaluation is itself
well established and is not a novelty claim of the present analysis.

Nor is it new for the forecasting specification to vary with the release
calendar. The six-date system of \citet{drechselmaurin2011flow} allows the
available indicators, equations, and aggregation weights to change together as
information accumulates. Contemporary policy applications go further:
\citet{moreira2025scmidas} develops a staggered-combination MIDAS architecture
that dynamically alters the contribution of hard and soft information through
the UK GDP release cycle. These precedents are particularly important for the
positioning of the present paper. They demonstrate that release-stage-specific
forecasting architectures already exist. The empirical issue developed below is
therefore narrower: whether the effect of receiving additional information can
be measured separately from the effect of changing the forecasting rule.

\subsection{Factor models, shrinkage, and high-dimensional information}
\label{subsec:lit_factor}
% ============================================================

A second challenge in macroeconomic nowcasting is dimensionality. Real-time
forecasters may observe dozens or hundreds of potentially relevant indicators,
while the available time-series sample is comparatively short. Factor methods
provide a canonical response by replacing a large predictor panel with a small
number of estimated common components. The diffusion-index and
principal-component framework of \citet{stockwatson2002principal} establishes
how information from many predictors can be compressed into a low-dimensional
forecasting representation. Dynamic-factor nowcasting subsequently became one
of the principal tools for processing large, ragged-edge macroeconomic datasets
\citep{giannone2008nowcasting,banbura2011blackbox}.

Shrinkage provides a different route to the same high-dimensional forecasting
problem. Rather than summarising the predictor panel before estimating the
forecast equation, shrinkage methods retain a potentially large set of
coefficients but restrict their effective complexity.
\citet{demol2008shrinkage} show that heavily regularised Bayesian regressions
using large predictor panels can generate forecasts closely related to
principal-component forecasts over a broad range of prior specifications.
Within multivariate systems, \citet{banbura2010largebvar} show that appropriately
scaled shrinkage can make large Bayesian VARs viable forecasting models even
when the number of coefficients would make unrestricted estimation impractical.

These results motivate treating factor extraction and shrinkage as competing
ways of controlling the information dimension rather than assuming that one
dominates universally. Modern forecasting comparisons further expand the set of
candidate procedures. \citet{kantpickwinter2025ml}, for example, compare
regularised regressions, factor-based approaches, and nonlinear
machine-learning methods in pseudo-real-time GDP nowcasting and find that their
relative performance changes across evaluation periods and forecast positions.
For the present study, the implication is not that high-dimensional or
regularised forecasting methods are new, but that heterogeneous model classes
provide an economically meaningful candidate set over which stage-dependent
relative performance can be investigated.

\subsection{Forecast combinations and model instability}
\label{subsec:lit_combinations}
% ============================================================

Uncertainty over the preferred specification has long provided a rationale for
forecast combination. As reviewed by \citet{timmermann2006combinations},
combining forecasts can reduce the risk associated with selecting a single
misspecified or unstable forecasting model, while simple weighting rules often
remain competitive with more highly estimated combinations. In a large
nowcasting comparison, \citet{kuzin2013pooling} document substantial variation
in the performance of individual specifications and show that the success of
model selection depends strongly on the selection rule, whereas pooling
provides comparatively stable performance. Forecast combinations therefore
form a natural benchmark against which any deterministic stage-dependent model
policy should be evaluated.

The underlying difficulty is broader than static model uncertainty.
Macroeconomic predictive relationships themselves can be unstable
\citep{stockwatson1996instability,rossi2021instability}. If relative forecast
performance varies systematically with observable conditions, forecast choice
may also be conditioned on those conditions. \citet{giacominiwhite2006conditional}
develop tests of conditional predictive ability precisely for settings in which
the relevant question is not only which forecast performs best on average, but
whether currently observed information can help determine which forecast is
likely to perform better at a future date. Their associated decision rule
provides an important econometric precursor to data-dependent forecast
selection.

A more explicit form of adaptation appears in dynamic model averaging and
selection. \citet{koopkorobilis2012dma} allow both coefficients and the identity
of the forecasting model to change through time in an application to U.S.
inflation. Model probabilities are updated recursively as new observations are
received, and dynamic model selection uses the specification with the highest
current probability. This literature makes clear that allowing the forecasting
model to change is not itself novel. It also highlights a distinction from the
design considered in this paper: recursive dynamic selection continually
updates the decision rule as the evaluation period progresses, whereas the
stage policy examined here is selected using a pre-evaluation development
sample and is then frozen before the final policy comparison.

The nowcasting literature brings model uncertainty and information arrival
together directly. \citet{aastveit2014density} show that model rankings change
through the U.S. GDP release cycle and that combining competing density
nowcasts can be more robust than selecting a single specification.
\citet{aastveit2018combined} subsequently allow combination weights to evolve
with past nowcasting performance and report especially large gains early in the
quarter, when uncertainty and data incompleteness are greatest. Together with
the stage-varying architectures of \citet{drechselmaurin2011flow} and
\citet{moreira2025scmidas}, this evidence rules out any broad claim that
stage-dependent selection, weighting, or adaptation is absent from the prior
literature. Instead, it motivates a more tightly controlled comparison of the
two mechanisms that can generate apparent stage-specific forecast gains:
additional information and a different forecasting rule.

\subsection{Research gap}
\label{subsec:research_gap}
% ============================================================

The preceding literature considerably narrows the defensible research gap.
Existing work already establishes that macroeconomic information arrives
sequentially, that publication lags affect the value of predictors, that forecast
accuracy and model rankings can change within the quarter, that forecasting
specifications and combination weights can vary across release stages, that
adaptive model selection can respond recursively to changing predictive
performance, and that historical data vintages can matter for forecast
evaluation
\citep{aastveit2014density,drechselmaurin2011flow,
giacominiwhite2006conditional,koopkorobilis2012dma,
heinrichreif2025realtime}. Related work also applies common mixed-frequency
architectures to activity, inflation, and labour-market variables
\citep{heinrichreif2025realtime}. Accordingly, the contribution of this paper
is neither ``stage-dependent forecasting'' in isolation nor the first use of a
real-time multi-target forecasting system.

The narrower gap concerns the \emph{experimental decomposition} of stage
effects. To our knowledge, the closest literature reviewed above does not
jointly impose the two controlled comparisons used here: first, a fixed-rule
cross-stage comparison that changes the information set while holding the
forecasting rule fixed; and second, a same-stage comparison between a
pre-specified stage policy and a fixed-model benchmark that holds the
information set fixed. Let
\begin{equation}
    \ell_{k,t,s}(m)
    =
    L\!\left(e_{k,t,s}^{(m)}\right)
\end{equation}
denote the loss for target $k$, forecast origin $t$, information stage $s$, and
forecasting rule $m$. The information-arrival contrast is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta^{I}_{k,t,s}(m)
    =
    \ell_{k,t,s+1}(m)
    -
    \ell_{k,t,s}(m),
    \label{eq:lit_information_contrast}
\end{equation}
where the forecasting rule is fixed and
$\It_{t,s}\rightarrow\It_{t,s+1}$. By contrast, the stage-policy comparison is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta^{P}_{k,t,s}
    =
    \ell_{k,t,s}\!\left(m^{*}_{k,s}\right)
    -
    \ell_{k,t,s}\!\left(m^{F}_{k}\right),
    \label{eq:lit_policy_contrast}
\end{equation}
where $\It_{t,s}$ is held fixed and only the forecasting rule changes. The
first comparison is a direct descendant of the real-time information-flow
literature
\citep{giannone2008nowcasting,schorfheide2015mixedfrequency,
liebermann2014realtime}; the second is related to conditional and adaptive
forecast selection
\citep{giacominiwhite2006conditional,koopkorobilis2012dma}.
What is distinctive is their use as sequentially nested counterfactuals within
one forecast experiment.

This decomposition is strengthened by two additional design restrictions.
First, the mapping from target and release stage to the stage-dependent rule,
$m^{*}_{k,s}$, is selected using a policy-development sample and frozen before
the final evaluation observations are used. The objective is therefore not to
show retrospectively which model happened to win at each stage, but to test
whether release stage contains sufficiently stable information about relative
model performance to support an ex-ante forecasting policy. Second, the
real-time-versus-latest-vintage experiment retains the same historical release
mask and changes only the vintage values that would populate that mask.
Consequently, the vintage comparison does not introduce observations that had
not yet been released and therefore does not confound the revision effect with
the information-arrival effect documented in the nowcasting literature
\citep{croushorestark2001realtime,starkcroushore2002forecasting,
koenig2003useabuse}. The resulting contribution is thus best characterised as a
disciplined pseudo-real-time comparison of forecasting policies: it separately
measures the predictive contribution of sequential information arrival, the
incremental performance of a pre-specified stage-dependent policy, and the
sensitivity of both conclusions to historically feasible data vintages across
heterogeneous GDP, inflation, and labour-market targets.
