\section{Robustness and Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:robustness}

\subsection{Robustness design and interpretation}
\label{subsec:robustness_design}

The analyses in this section are secondary robustness exercises rather than
additional confirmatory hypotheses. Their design was frozen after completion
of H1--H4 and before the robustness results were computed. The confirmatory
evaluation samples, loss conventions, stage definitions, frozen research
policy, and H4 no-look-ahead rule are therefore unchanged. No confirmatory
output is regenerated or replaced. The robustness exercises instead ask
whether the interpretation of the main findings is sensitive to five
predeclared dimensions: the fixed model used in H1, the length of the
development period used to select the H2 policy, stage-level heterogeneity in
the archived H3 vintage comparison, the memory parameter used for H4 interval
calibration, and multiplicity adjustment of the archived confirmatory
\(p\)-values.

This distinction is important for interpretation. A robustness result can
show that the qualitative conclusion survives an alternative specification
without promoting that alternative specification to the status of a new
primary model. Conversely, sensitivity of a particular cell or tuning
parameter does not retroactively invalidate the frozen confirmatory result; it
identifies where the evidence is specification dependent and where subsequent
applications should be cautious. Table~\ref{tab:robustness_overview}
summarises the five exercises.

\subsection{Alternative H1 fixed model}
\label{subsec:h1_alternative_models}

The confirmatory H1 experiment intentionally holds one development-selected
fixed model constant within each target. R1 asks whether the information-
arrival conclusion depends on that particular comparator. For each target,
the alternative model is the runner-up by pooled development-sample MSE after
excluding the primary fixed comparator, with MAE and then model name used as
the predeclared tie breakers. Selection uses the same common development
sample as the primary comparator and does not use evaluation-period
performance. The resulting alternatives are Bridge--DFM Ensemble for GDP,
Ridge--AR Ensemble for headline CPI, AR(1) for core CPI, Ridge--AR Ensemble
for both PCE targets, Equal-Weight Ensemble for average hourly earnings,
12-Month Mean for PAYEMS, and AR(1) for UNRATE.

Under these alternative fixed models, the later information set has lower
mean squared loss in 20 of the 28 adjacent-stage comparisons, higher loss in
7, and exactly the same loss in one. The squared-loss sign agrees with the
primary H1 result in 18 of 28 transitions. Five alternative-model transitions
are significant at the unadjusted 5\% level: four improvements and one
deterioration. The improvements are GDP after month 1 to after month 2
(\(p=0.0359\)), GDP quarter end to pre-advance release (\(p=0.0424\)),
core CPI month open to mid-month (\(p=0.0185\)), and core PCE mid-month to
month end (\(p=0.0088\)). The deterioration is GDP early quarter to after
month 1 (\(p=0.00065\)).

The robustness result therefore preserves the broad non-monotonic H1
interpretation while weakening any attempt to attach too much importance to an
individual transition. In particular, the primary UNRATE month-end to
pre-employment-report improvement is no longer significant with the runner-up
AR(1) comparator, whereas several GDP transitions become significant. What
is stable is the non-monotonic structure: changing only the fixed model does
not produce an ordering in which every later stage is more accurate. The
marginal predictive value of incoming information remains conditional on both
the target and the forecasting model.

\subsection{Policy-development stability}
\label{subsec:policy_development_stability}

R2 shortens the policy-development sample while leaving the evaluation sample
unchanged. The alternative development endpoint is 2022Q2 for GDP and June
2022 for inflation and labour. Candidate models, common-sample restrictions,
MSE--MAE--model-name selection rules, and the target-level fixed-comparator
rule are otherwise identical to the primary policy freeze. Observations
between the shortened development endpoint and the confirmatory evaluation
start are not used to choose the alternative policy.

The selected policy is highly stable. Only 2 of the 36 target--stage mappings
change, and none of the 8 target-level fixed comparators changes. The two
changes occur in inflation: headline PCE at month open switches from the
Ridge--AR Ensemble to Bridge Ridge, while core PCE at mid-month switches from
the Ridge--AR Ensemble to Bridge Ridge. In both cases the shortened-sample
choice becomes identical to the target-level fixed comparator, so those cells
become mechanical ties rather than informative H2 comparisons. The
shortened-development exercise therefore contains 9 informative cells instead
of the primary 11.

All nine remaining informative cells retain the same sign of the mean
squared-loss differential as in the primary H2 evaluation. Four policy gains
remain significant at the unadjusted 5\% level and there are again no
significant policy losses: the Equal-Weight Ensemble for average hourly
earnings at month end and before the employment report, the 12-Month Mean for
PAYEMS at month open, and AR(1) for UNRATE at month open. The GDP policy
departures remain positive but statistically weak, while the headline and core
CPI month-open policy gains remain negative but statistically weak.

This exercise supports the labour-market interpretation of H2. The strongest
labour-market policy gains persist when July--December 2022 is excluded from
policy development. At the same time, the two inflation mapping changes show
why a frozen ex-ante policy is preferable to presenting a continually
reselected policy as if it were uniquely determined by the data.

\subsection{Vintage-effect heterogeneity across stages}
\label{subsec:h3_stage_heterogeneity}

R3 does not generate new H3 forecasts. It uses only the archived stage-level
real-time versus latest-vintage comparisons and therefore preserves the
confirmatory H3 information mask, outcomes, selected models, and loss
definition. As in Section~\ref{subsec:h3_results}, a negative differential
means that latest-vintage data lower measured loss.

The stage-level results clarify which target-level effects are broad and which
are concentrated. For GDP, the mean squared-loss differential is negative at
all five stages, although no individual stage rejects equality at the 5\%
level. Headline CPI and core CPI are also negative at all four stages in
squared loss; core CPI additionally has negative absolute-loss differentials
at all four stages. These patterns show that the negative target-level signs
for GDP and core CPI are not produced by a single release stage, even though
their target-level statistical evidence remains weaker than for
unemployment.

The PCE measures provide the counterexample. Headline PCE favours
latest-vintage data in squared loss at only one of four stages. Core PCE has
positive mean squared- and absolute-loss differentials at all four stages:
using latest-vintage predictors makes the historical exercise worse throughout
the release cycle rather than only at one endpoint. Average hourly earnings
and PAYEMS each favour latest-vintage data at four of five stages but without a
uniformly significant stage pattern.

UNRATE is the strongest case. Latest-vintage data lower squared loss at all
five stages and lower absolute loss at four of five. Four of the five
stage-level squared-loss comparisons and four of the five absolute-loss
comparisons are individually significant at 5\%. Hence the very small
target-level H3 \(p\)-values for UNRATE are not driven by a single stage. R3
therefore reinforces the confirmatory conclusion that revision effects are
target specific: some are broad across the release calendar, but their
direction is not common across macroeconomic variables.

\subsection{Interval-calibration memory}
\label{subsec:h4_interval_robustness}

R4 retains the H4 nominal 80\% coverage target, the same target--stage--model
calibration scope, the strict requirement that the calibration outcome be
released before the current forecast date, the same minimum-history
requirements, and no fallback rule. It perturbs only the memory parameter.
For GDP, rolling-\(q_{0.8}\) windows of 12 and 32 observations are compared
with the frozen 20-observation window. For inflation, exponential half-lives
of 12 and 24 months are compared with the frozen half-life of 18. For labour,
decay factors of 0.90 and 0.97 are compared with the frozen value of 0.94.
The paired interval-score differential is alternative minus frozen-primary, so
negative values favour the robustness variant.

GDP is the clearest sensitivity finding. Both alternative windows have lower
mean interval scores than the frozen 20-observation rule at every stage.
Nine of the ten comparisons reject equality at the unadjusted 5\% level; the
only exception is the 12-observation window at the early-quarter stage
(\(p=0.0738\)). The 32-observation window significantly improves the score at
all five stages. This result is consistent with the confirmatory diagnosis of conservative
GDP intervals and preserves the prior-only calibration architecture, but it
also shows that the exact GDP memory length is consequential. The frozen
20-observation window should therefore not be interpreted as empirically
unique or optimal.

Inflation is much less sensitive. Across 32 target--stage--memory
comparisons, none of the half-life-12 or half-life-24 score differentials is
significant at 5\%. Signs vary across targets and stages, but there is no
evidence that modestly faster or slower exponential decay overturns the
inflation results. The frozen half-life of 18 therefore appears to be a
reasonable representative choice within the predeclared local sensitivity
range.

Labour lies between these cases. Average hourly earnings has no significant
memory perturbation. PAYEMS is notably sensitive to slower decay: the
0.97-decay variant has significantly higher interval scores at all five
stages, while faster decay significantly improves the pre-employment-report
score. For UNRATE, faster decay improves month-open performance, whereas
slower decay worsens month open but modestly improves the
pre-employment-report score. Thus the H4 labour result that adaptive weighting
can be valuable is robust, but the preferred memory can itself be
target- and stage-dependent.

\subsection{Multiplicity-adjusted inference}
\label{subsec:multiplicity}

The confirmatory tests are reported with their predeclared unadjusted
two-sided \(p\)-values because the paper's estimands are target--stage
specific. R5 additionally applies Holm's step-down family-wise correction
within nine predeclared families. This is a sensitivity analysis of the
strength of individual significance statements, not a replacement of the
confirmatory inference.

The adjustment materially narrows the cell-level point-forecast evidence. For
H1, the only squared- and absolute-loss result that survives is the UNRATE
month-end to pre-employment-report improvement (Holm \(p=0.0351\) for squared
loss and \(0.0387\) for absolute loss). For H2 squared loss, the two
average-hourly-earnings gains at month end and before the employment report
survive, as does the UNRATE month-open gain; the PAYEMS month-open result no
longer survives family-wise correction. For H2 absolute loss, only the
UNRATE month-open gain remains significant. For H3, the UNRATE result
survives under both squared and absolute loss, whereas the core-PCE
absolute-loss deterioration does not.

For H4 unconditional coverage, only the severe UNRATE month-open overcoverage
survives Holm adjustment. The interval-score evidence is less dependent on
unadjusted significance: 17 of the 36 primary-versus-Gaussian comparisons and
9 of the 31 primary-versus-rolling comparisons remain significant after
family-wise correction. These counts include both favourable and
unfavourable score differences and therefore should not be read as a pooled
``win rate.'' They show instead that many of the method-ranking differences
are large enough to remain statistically distinguishable even under a
conservative multiplicity correction.

\subsection{Robustness synthesis}
\label{subsec:robustness_synthesis}

The robustness exercises sharpen rather than reverse the main conclusions.
First, H1 remains non-monotonic when the fixed comparator is replaced by a
development-sample runner-up, although the identity of the individually
significant transition is model sensitive. Second, the H2 policy mapping is stable under this perturbation:
shortening the development sample changes only two inflation policy mappings,
leaves every fixed comparator unchanged, and preserves the sign of every
remaining informative squared-loss comparison. The strongest policy evidence
continues to come from labour.

Third, the H3 stage decomposition shows that the two clearest target-level
directions are not single-stage artefacts: UNRATE improves under
latest-vintage information throughout the release cycle, whereas core PCE
worsens throughout it. Fourth, H4 is robust at the level of its general
message but not every tuning parameter. Inflation is locally insensitive to
the decay perturbation, while GDP and some labour targets reveal economically
meaningful memory sensitivity. Finally, Holm correction removes many
marginal cell-level significance claims but leaves the strongest UNRATE and
average-hourly-earnings results, together with a substantial set of
probabilistic score differences.

Accordingly, the robustness evidence does not justify a stronger claim of
universal stage dependence or universal superiority of one forecasting or
interval method. It supports the narrower conclusion developed throughout
the paper: release-stage information, model choice, data vintage, and
uncertainty calibration interact in target-specific ways, and a credible
real-time forecasting system should make those information boundaries and
selection rules explicit rather than treating them as hidden implementation
details.
