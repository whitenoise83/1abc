\section{Economic Interpretation and Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why forecast performance changes across information stages}
\label{subsec:why_stage_changes}

The central empirical result is that a later position in the release calendar
does not mechanically imply a better nowcast.  In H1, where the forecasting
rule is held fixed, later-stage mean squared loss is lower in 16 of 28 adjacent
comparisons, but the statistically distinguishable movements include both
improvements and deteriorations.  This pattern is economically plausible in a
ragged-edge environment.  A new release changes the information set, but the
marginal signal contained in that release may be small, noisy, redundant with
information already observed, or imperfectly mapped into the target by a model
whose coefficients were estimated over an unstable historical relationship.
Consequently, the value of an additional observation depends on both its
timing and the information that precedes it.  This interpretation is consistent
with the nowcasting literature's emphasis on publication lags and the changing
marginal contribution of hard, soft, and financial indicators through the
release cycle \citep{banbura2011blackbox,liebermann2014realtime}.

The H1 results therefore sharpen the usual statement that forecasts are
updated as information arrives.  What matters empirically is not the amount of
calendar time that has elapsed but whether the newly observable information
improves the conditional forecast.  The significant gains for core CPI and the
PCE measures around particular intra-month transitions, and for unemployment
immediately before the employment report, coexist with significant
deteriorations at other transitions.  The latter are not anomalous under the
experimental design: because the model is deliberately fixed, a deterioration
is evidence that the richer information set does not necessarily translate
into lower realised loss for that rule and sample.  This finding is closely
related to evidence that relative forecast performance can change as the
release calendar progresses \citep{aastveit2014density}.

H2 adds a second layer.  Once the information set is held fixed, changing the
forecasting rule can sometimes add predictive value, but the evidence is much
more selective than a universal stage-switching argument would imply.  The
frozen stage policy differs from the fixed comparator in only 11 of 36
target--stage cells, and all four statistically significant gains occur in the
labour domain.  Thus the release stage appears to contain useful information
about model choice in some settings, but most cells either retain the same
development-selected model or do not show a statistically distinguishable
gain from switching.  The appropriate interpretation is conditional rather
than global: stage dependence can be exploitable when relative model
performance is sufficiently stable across the development and evaluation
samples, but it should not be imposed merely because the information set has
changed.

The interval results reinforce this interpretation.  H4 shows that uncertainty
also evolves differently across stages.  GDP's primary rolling empirical
interval is conservative throughout the evaluation sample, while the relative
interval-score ranking against the Gaussian benchmark reverses as the quarter
matures.  Unemployment coverage moves from substantial overcoverage at month
open to undercoverage after week 2 and at month end before returning to the
nominal rate immediately before the employment report.  The robustness analysis further shows that the exact GDP calibration
memory is consequential: both 12- and 32-observation windows lower mean
interval scores relative to the frozen 20-observation rule at every stage, and
the 32-observation window improves significantly at all five stages. By
contrast, none of the 32 inflation half-life perturbations is significant at
5\%. A stage-aware forecasting system therefore needs to treat both the
conditional mean and its uncertainty as release-calendar objects rather than
assuming a single time-invariant performance profile, and it should avoid
treating one calibration-memory choice as universally optimal.

\subsection{Differences across GDP, inflation, and labour markets}
\label{subsec:cross_domain}

The cross-domain results are deliberately heterogeneous.  GDP provides the
smallest confirmatory sample, with 12 evaluation quarters per stage.  H1 does
not reveal a robust monotone gain as the quarter advances, and the H2
development policy's two departures from the fixed Dynamic Factor Model do not
produce statistically distinguishable squared-loss improvements.  This should
not be read as evidence that within-quarter information is unimportant for GDP.
Rather, it indicates that, in this evaluation sample and candidate set, the
incremental information and model-switching effects are difficult to estimate
precisely.  The GDP interval evidence makes the finite-sample issue especially
visible: 100\% empirical coverage sounds favourable in isolation, but the
unconditional-coverage test rejects the nominal 80\% rate and the resulting
intervals can be too wide to dominate the Gaussian benchmark later in the
quarter.

Inflation presents a different pattern.  The information-arrival experiment
contains several statistically meaningful transitions, but the frozen
stage-policy substitutions are not themselves significant on squared loss.
This distinction matters because it shows why changes in forecast accuracy
across a release cycle should not automatically be attributed to changing
models.  For these targets, much of the economically relevant movement appears
to come from the evolving information set itself.  At the same time, H4 shows
that model uncertainty and forecast uncertainty need not have the same
structure: the exponentially weighted empirical interval performs especially
well for core CPI even though the corresponding H2 stage-policy changes do not
establish broad point-forecast superiority.  Point forecasting and interval
calibration therefore provide related but distinct information about forecast
quality.

The labour results are the strongest evidence in favour of stage-dependent
policy design.  The significant H2 gains for average hourly earnings,
PAYEMS, and unemployment occur at different points in the monthly release
cycle and involve different models.  H3 also identifies unemployment as the
target for which replacing real-time values by the frozen latest-vintage
counterfactual most clearly changes measured forecast accuracy.  H4, in turn,
finds large interval-score gains for earnings and payroll employment and a
strongly stage-dependent unemployment coverage profile.  One interpretation
is that the labour domain provides a particularly rich setting in which timely
within-month indicators, persistence, and rapidly changing conditions can
alter the relative usefulness of simple, bridge, factor, and combination
rules.  The experiment does not identify a causal mechanism for those gains,
so this explanation should be regarded as an economic interpretation rather
than a tested structural claim.

The contrast across domains is consistent with the broader forecasting
literature, which finds that the relative performance of model classes and
combinations is sensitive to the target, forecast position, and economic
environment \citep{timmermann2006combinations,kuzin2013pooling,
kantpickwinter2025ml}.  The present evidence therefore argues against
transporting a successful rule from one macroeconomic target to another
without a fresh real-time evaluation.

\subsection{Information-set construction versus model sophistication}
\label{subsec:information_vs_model}

The decomposition between H1 and H2 provides a useful perspective on the role
of model sophistication.  H1 changes the information set while fixing the
model; H2 holds the information set fixed while changing the model according
to a pre-specified stage policy.  Because H1 identifies meaningful changes in
loss even without model switching, and because 25 of 36 H2 cells are
same-model ties, a substantial part of stage-dependent forecast behaviour is
attributable to information-set construction rather than to continual changes
in specification.  This does not make model choice unimportant.  The
significant labour-market H2 gains show that the forecasting rule can matter
materially.  It does imply, however, that sophisticated model selection cannot
substitute for reconstructing the data that were actually observable at the
forecast origin.

H3 strengthens that conclusion.  Its latest-vintage counterfactual preserves
the historical availability mask and changes the values populating that mask.
The fact that measured forecast loss changes---strongly for unemployment and
in the opposite direction for some PCE metrics---shows that even an unchanged
model operating on an unchanged observation pattern can be evaluated
differently when revised values replace historically available ones.  This is
precisely why the real-time-data literature treats vintage construction as
part of the econometric experiment rather than as a clerical preprocessing
choice \citep{croushorestark2001realtime,starkcroushore2002forecasting,
koenig2003useabuse}.

The same discipline applies to probabilistic forecasting.  The H4 calibration
history admits an error only after its corresponding outcome release date is
strictly earlier than the current forecast date.  Without that gate, an
apparently sophisticated empirical interval could use information that a
historical forecaster did not yet possess.  Across point and interval
forecasting, the more general lesson is therefore methodological: the validity
of a real-time comparison depends first on the information boundary, and only
then on the forecasting algorithm operating inside that boundary.

\subsection{Stable policies versus continual model switching}
\label{subsec:policy_stability}

The stage policy in this paper is intentionally more conservative than a fully
adaptive model-selection scheme.  The target--stage mapping is estimated on
the policy-development sample and then frozen before confirmatory evaluation.
This design sacrifices the possibility of immediately exploiting recent
forecast performance in exchange for a cleaner out-of-sample test of whether
release stage contains persistent information about relative model
performance.  It therefore differs from dynamic model averaging or selection,
where model probabilities are recursively updated as new observations and
forecast errors arrive \citep{koopkorobilis2012dma}.

The H2 evidence suggests why that distinction matters.  A flexible adaptive
algorithm could always create more opportunities to switch, but the
confirmatory experiment finds reliable gains only in a subset of target--stage
cells.  Continual switching would therefore face a bias--variance trade-off:
it might respond faster when the forecasting environment genuinely changes,
but it could also chase short-lived ranking noise.  The frozen-policy design
cannot determine which side of that trade-off dominates; it establishes a
stable benchmark against which a prior-only adaptive challenger can later be
evaluated.

The robustness evidence strengthens the case for treating the frozen policy as
a stable benchmark rather than an arbitrary sample artefact. Shortening the
development period changes only 2 of 36 stage-policy mappings and none of the
8 target-level fixed comparators, while all 9 remaining informative H2 cells
retain the sign of the primary squared-loss differential. Holm adjustment
narrows the individually strongest H2 squared-loss evidence from four
nominally significant labour cells to three: the two average-hourly-earnings
gains and the UNRATE month-open gain survive, whereas the PAYEMS month-open
gain does not. Development-window stability is therefore strong, but the
multiplicity exercise supports a narrower interpretation of which individual
cells carry the most statistical weight.

This perspective also connects to conditional predictive-ability methods.
Observable state variables can, in principle, help determine which forecast
will perform better \citep{giacominiwhite2006conditional}.  Release stage is
one such observable state variable, but the present results show that its
usefulness for model choice is target dependent.  A natural extension is
therefore not unrestricted ex-post switching, but a strictly prior-only
adaptive rule whose updating speed and complexity are themselves fixed before
evaluation.  Such an exercise would test whether recent relative performance
adds information beyond the stable stage mapping without weakening the
real-time boundary.

\subsection{Practical implications}
\label{subsec:implications}

For operational nowcasting systems, the findings point to a hierarchy of
design priorities.  First, the system should preserve timestamped historical
data and an explicit release calendar so that each forecast can be reproduced
from the information actually observable at its origin.  Second, forecast
monitoring should be stage specific: an error measured at month open is not
necessarily comparable to an error measured immediately before release, even
when both forecasts target the same outcome.  Third, stage-dependent model
choice should be introduced only where development-sample evidence is
sufficiently stable to justify it; otherwise a fixed or combined benchmark can
remain preferable.  Finally, interval calibration should be audited separately
from point forecasting because high coverage can simply reflect excessive
width.

These implications are relevant to central-bank, government, and private-sector
forecasting workflows, but the paper does not evaluate policy decisions,
trading outcomes, or welfare consequences.  The evidence supports procedures
for constructing and evaluating real-time forecasts; it does not imply that
the statistically preferred forecast at a particular stage must generate a
materially better downstream decision.  Users of such systems should therefore
separate forecast-performance evidence from the economic value of acting on
the forecast.

A practical benefit of the frozen-policy approach is governance.  A production
system can record which model is authorised for each target and release stage,
which historical observations entered the forecast, and which prior errors
were eligible for interval calibration.  Changes to the policy can then be
evaluated as explicit model-governance decisions rather than being confounded
with silent changes in data vintages or release timing.  This auditability is
particularly valuable when the objective is reproducible policy or risk
analysis rather than retrospective leaderboard performance.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

Several limitations constrain the strength and generality of the conclusions.
The first is sample size.  The GDP confirmatory evaluation contains only 12
quarters per stage, while the monthly targets contain roughly 38--41
observations.  HAC tests, violation-independence tests, and comparisons of
interval coverage consequently have limited finite-sample power in some cells.
The GDP result is especially sensitive to this constraint.  In addition, the
paper reports many target--stage comparisons. The confirmatory cell-level
\(p\)-values remain the predeclared unadjusted tests for target--stage
estimands; the secondary Holm exercise shows that family-wise adjustment
substantially reduces the number of individually significant H1--H3 cells.
The strongest UNRATE results and the two late-stage average-hourly-earnings H2
gains are comparatively robust, but marginal cell-level findings should not be
read as evidence for a universal stage effect.

Second, the results are conditional on the candidate-model universe and on the
pre-specified stage definitions.  The experiment includes persistence,
trailing-mean, bridge, ridge, factor, and combination specifications that span
important forecasting approaches, but it does not exhaust nonlinear,
large-scale Bayesian, or modern machine-learning alternatives.  Likewise, the
chosen stages represent economically meaningful points in the release calendar
rather than a claim that no alternative partition could be informative.

Third, H3 is a deliberately narrow counterfactual.  It holds forecast origins
and the real-time observation mask fixed and replaces values with a frozen
latest-vintage extract where available.  It therefore isolates a revision
channel more cleanly than a fully revised rectangular dataset would, but it is
not identical to a universal first-release-versus-final-release experiment for
every underlying series and date.  The secondary stage-level decomposition
shows whether the archived vintage effect is broad or concentrated across
release stages, but candidate-model ranking sensitivity remains outside the
present analysis.

Fourth, the H4 intervals are symmetric around the point forecast and use
domain-specific empirical calibration rules.  They do not model asymmetric
tail risk, forecast bias, or state-dependent skewness directly.  Coverage and
independence tests are also difficult to interpret with short sequences, and
the GDP case demonstrates that nominal-looking coverage metrics must be read
together with width and proper scoring rules.

Finally, the application is U.S.-specific and spans an unusually unstable
period that includes the COVID-19 shock and its aftermath.  That instability
is economically relevant to the forecasting problem, but it may also make
relative model performance less portable to more tranquil samples or to other
economies.  External validation across countries, alternative historical
episodes, and prospectively accumulated post-study observations is therefore
necessary before treating the estimated stage policies as generally optimal.
